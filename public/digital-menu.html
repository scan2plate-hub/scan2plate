A printed menu is out of date the day it arrives

Tomato prices move. A supplier misses a delivery. A dish that sounded good stops selling. None of that waits for a reprint.

So the card gets patched. A sticker over the old rate, a pen line through the paneer, a waiter who tells every table that two things are finished. Guests read a menu that is partly fiction and order something that arrives as an apology.

Reprinting properly costs money and takes days, which is why most restaurants do it once a year and live with the patches in between.

How putting your menu online works

  1. Build the menu once. Add items with a name, price and category. Categories control the order guests see, so starters come before desserts without renumbering anything.
  2. Add the detail that answers questions. Mark items veg or non-veg, set separate half and full prices where you serve both, and add a photograph for the dishes people hesitate over.
  3. Change a price in one place. Edit the item and the new rate applies to the next order taken, at every table and at the counter.
  4. Mark a dish unavailable when it runs out. It stops being orderable immediately instead of being sold and then apologised for.
  5. Guests read it from the table QR code in their own browser, or your staff read the same menu on the POS. One menu, not two that drift apart.

What a live menu changes

Three things stop happening.

Selling what you do not have. Marking an item unavailable takes a moment during service, and removes the worst version of a guest interaction: an order accepted and then withdrawn.

Arguments about the price. The rate on the guest's phone is the rate on the bill, because both read the same record. There is no printed card saying something different.

Photography being a luxury. A dish with a photograph is easier to order than one described in four words, particularly for a guest who does not know the cuisine. Adding one costs nothing per item.

Getting your existing menu in

The work is typing the items once, and it is the same work whichever system you choose.

Most restaurants start with the categories they already use on the printed card, add the items under each, and leave photographs until later. A hundred-item menu takes an afternoon. After that, changes are per-item and take seconds, which is the point: the cost is paid once and the menu never goes stale again.

Who this suits, and who it does not

It suits you if your prices move with your costs, you run out of things during service, or your menu has been patched since the last reprint.

It suits you less if the printed card is part of the experience you are selling — a fine-dining room where the menu is an object. A digital menu can sit alongside one, but it will not replace it, and it should not.

Questions restaurant owners ask about a digital menu

How quickly does a price change reach guests?

It applies to the next order taken. A guest whose phone already has the menu open sees the new price when they refresh or reopen it, and the bill is calculated from the stored item, so the amount charged is always the current rate rather than a cached one.

Can I show half and full portions with different prices?

Yes. An item can carry a separate half price and full price, and the guest picks which they want when ordering. The bill and the kitchen ticket both show which portion was chosen, so the kitchen is not guessing from the amount charged.

Do I have to add photographs?

No, they are optional and the menu works without them. Items with no photograph show a neutral placeholder rather than a broken image. Most restaurants add pictures gradually, starting with the dishes guests most often ask about before ordering.

Does the printed menu become useless?

Not necessarily. Many restaurants keep a printed card for guests who prefer one and use the digital menu for pricing, availability and QR ordering. The point is that the digital version is the one that is correct, so the printed card stops being the source of truth.
