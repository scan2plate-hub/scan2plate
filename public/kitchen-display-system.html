The spike is a single point of failure

In most kitchens the record of what has been ordered is a row of paper slips on a metal spike, in roughly the order they arrived.

It works until a slip is put on the wrong spike, falls behind the counter, or is written in handwriting the person now cooking cannot read. When that happens nobody discovers it from the kitchen. They discover it when a table asks where their food is, twenty-five minutes later, and the answer has to be reconstructed.

The other cost is the interruption. A waiter who wants to know whether table 7 is ready has to walk in and ask, which stops whoever is asked.

How an order reaching the kitchen screen works

  1. The order arrives as a ticket on screen. Whether it came from a waiter on the POS or from a guest scanning the table QR code, it appears in the kitchen immediately, typed rather than handwritten.
  2. It carries its table or token number. The kitchen always knows where the food is going without checking anywhere else.
  3. The kitchen moves it through states. An order runs from pending, to preparing, to ready, to served. Touching the screen moves it on.
  4. The floor reads the same states. Waiters see what is ready without walking in and interrupting.
  5. Added rounds appear as their own tickets. A second round from the same table arrives as a new ticket, because that is what the kitchen needs to cook, even though the bill stays single.

What the states are actually for

Four states sound like bureaucracy until a rush starts.

Pending says nobody has begun, which is the state that matters when a table has been waiting. Preparing stops two cooks starting the same dish. Ready is what a waiter reads from the floor rather than by asking. Served clears it from the screen so the board shows only live work.

The screen also answers the question a spike never can: what has been waiting longest. Order of arrival is visible rather than inferred from where a slip happens to sit.

Where to put the screen

The screen belongs where the person assembling plates can read it without turning round, which is usually the pass rather than the cooking line.

Heat, steam and oil are the practical constraints. A tablet on a wall bracket a metre back from the range survives; one propped beside a tandoor does not. Restaurants running two stations sometimes use two screens, or keep a printed ticket for the station furthest from the pass. Both work, and neither costs extra.

Who this suits, and who it does not

It suits you if more than one person cooks, orders arrive faster than they are finished at peak, or you have lost a ticket and found out from the guest.

It suits you less if your kitchen has nowhere safe to mount a screen, or the cooking area is wet and hot enough that a tablet will not survive. A printed KOT is the better answer there, and it is included at the same price — this is not a feature you have to take.

Questions restaurant owners ask about a kitchen display

Do I still need a KOT printer?

Not necessarily, but you can keep one. Many restaurants run both: the screen for tracking what is live, and a printed ticket for a station that cannot see the screen. Printing continues to work offline, which the live screen does not, so some kitchens keep a printer for that reason alone.

What screen do I need to buy?

Anything that runs a browser. An Android tablet propped where the pass can see it is the commonest choice, and an old monitor on a spare PC works equally well. There is no dedicated kitchen terminal to buy and no per-screen charge.

How does the kitchen know which table an order is for?

The table or token number is on the ticket, carried automatically from wherever the order was placed. A guest scanning table 7's QR code and a waiter opening table 7 on the POS both produce a ticket marked table 7, so the kitchen never has to ask.

What happens to an order that is cancelled after cooking starts?

It is marked cancelled and leaves the live board, so nobody continues cooking it. The bill number for a cancelled bill is retired rather than reused, which keeps the day's billing sequence explainable even when orders change during service.
